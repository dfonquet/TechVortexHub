Design Context

This design connected two campus buildings. In the original layout, Building 1 provided the ISP handoffs, C9300 StackWise aggregation, and FortiGate 600-series HA firewalls. Building 2 used two C9500 switches in StackWise Virtual as a combined core/distribution layer on Floor 1, with OM3 fiber extending to the Floor 5 access zones and a local Floor 1 extension. The firewall was intended to control traffic between the campus and the Internet; a physical connection to the aggregation stack alone does not enforce that boundary.

I put particular care into the fiber path, firewall handoff, switching roles, and documentation. Those decisions determine how a fault is isolated and how safely the network can grow. This case study preserves the original design and material notes while identifying gaps that would need to be resolved before treating them as an implementation plan.

Connectivity Options

The design decision centered on the separation between ISP handoffs, firewall policy, campus distribution, and the inter-building fiber path. Each choice affects both the failure domain and the work required to operate the network.

The options below describe those trade-offs. The single fiber run is a physical constraint, separate from the choice of WAN aggregation topology.

Option Strength Trade-off
Shared WAN aggregation and campus transit switching Simple physical design and fewer intermediate devices. Shares a switching failure domain and requires strict VLAN and routing separation to prevent firewall bypass.
Dedicated WAN aggregation stack upstream of the firewall Separates ISP transport from trusted campus switching and simplifies tracing the firewall handoff. Adds hardware and management overhead; it still needs resilient links and power.
Single OM3 run from Floor 1 to Floor 5 access rack Clear and easy to document when the path is known and controlled. A cable or shared-route failure can disconnect all three Floor 5 zones. Additional strands on the same route do not remove that risk.

Proposed Architecture

The physical drawing shows connectivity, but it is not a forwarding or security-policy map. For the intended Internet boundary, the logical path must be: ISP handoff → isolated WAN VLAN on the Building 1 stack → FortiGate WAN interface → firewall routing and policy → trusted LAN transit → C9500 core/distribution → access switches. Campus-originated Internet traffic follows the reverse direction through the firewall. SD-WAN selects among WAN members; its zone is a logical construct on the FortiGate, not a separate transit device.

If the Building 1 C9300 stack carries both ISP VLANs and the trusted campus transit, those segments must remain separate. No SVI, route, or Layer 2 bridge may provide a direct WAN-to-campus bypass. The OM3 rack is a passive patching point, not a security boundary. The article does not include the VLAN, gateway, routing, or firewall configuration needed to verify enforcement. That verification must include return paths and failover behavior. Inter-VLAN traffic routed locally on the C9500 pair does not automatically cross the FortiGate; any segment requiring stateful inspection needs a forwarding path through it.

On the campus side, the C9500 pair connects through the Floor 1 patching rack to the Floor 5 rack and the local Floor 1 extension. The diagram groups Floor 5 into D1, D2, and D3, each with two four-switch C9300 stacks, plus one four-switch C9300 stack on Floor 1. These are the diagram's allocations, not a reconciliation of the separate C9200L/C9300 material list. The single documented Floor 1-to-Floor 5 OM3 run is shared by all three access zones.

Transceiver and Media Plan

Fiber type, measured path length, loss budget, patching, and platform support determine optic selection. The diagram's OM3 paths are not automatically compatible with every optic in the material list: GLC-LH-SMD has a 10 km single-mode rating and separate multimode requirements, while SFP-10G-LR requires single-mode fiber. For a proposed 10G OM3 link, a supported multimode optic such as 10GBASE-SR would need validation at both endpoints. This is a design consideration, not a claim that SR optics or 10G links were installed.

Resiliency Decisions

Device redundancy does not establish end-to-end physical redundancy. The single OM3 run between Floors 1 and 5 introduces a physical single point of failure for all three Floor 5 zones. Formal risk acceptance and a recovery target are not documented. A more resilient implementation would use independently routed fiber paths; parallel links sharing the same cable, riser, or patching dependency cannot provide that protection.

  • Firewall HA: A pair can protect against a unit failure, but does not remove shared switching, power, or fiber dependencies. HA heartbeat, monitored interfaces, session handling, and failover must be validated for the actual model, mode, and FortiOS release.
  • StackWise Virtual: The C9500 pair operates as one logical switch. A Multi-Chassis EtherChannel (MEC) distributes a port-channel's members across both chassis; it is not the StackWise Virtual Link (SVL). The SVL and dual-active detection design need separate validation.
  • Stack failure domains: StackWise simplifies management but retains shared software and configuration risks. The diagram's two stacks per Floor 5 zone limit the scope of a stack outage only if their uplinks are independent; the shared fiber run still affects both.
  • Power and paths: Redundant supplies and multiple links help only within their actual failure domains. Independent power feeds, patching, and inter-building route diversity are not established by the drawing.
  • Operational validation: Label both ends of each link and test member-link loss, switch failure, firewall failover, and loss of the shared fiber path. Record reachability, session impact, remaining capacity, and recovery behavior against agreed acceptance criteria.

Security and Layer 2 Controls

The original design called for 802.1X, VLAN segmentation, ACLs, storm control, PortFast, and BPDU Guard. These controls address different risks: VLANs separate broadcast domains; ACLs enforce selected traffic restrictions; firewall policy provides stateful inspection where traffic crosses the FortiGate. The record does not establish their deployed configuration. From an operational perspective, gateway placement, allowed VLANs, and policy ownership must be explicit so troubleshooting does not confuse segmentation with firewall enforcement.

  • 802.1X for authenticated access.
  • VLAN segmentation for user, voice, management, cameras, and access systems.
  • Storm control with thresholds appropriate to the endpoint and traffic profile.
  • PortFast on endpoint-facing ports, not as a substitute for loop prevention.
  • BPDU Guard to protect the spanning-tree edge.
  • ACLs for controlled inter-segment access.

Growth Planning

The design considered additional users, wireless access, cameras, access-control systems, and cabling. Capacity planning should account for free ports, PoE budget, fiber pairs, uplink utilization, and the capacity remaining after a failure. No measured demand or growth target is recorded here, so sufficient headroom cannot be claimed. The proposed C9300 module upgrade provides a potential distribution growth path; it does not upgrade the C9200L-48P-4G's fixed 1G uplinks. That distinction matters when assessing oversubscription and deciding which layer needs investment.

Lessons Learned

  • A useful design aligns the topology, BOM, port schedule, and security policy; conflicts between them must remain visible until resolved.
  • Fiber paths, rack locations, and power planning are part of network resiliency.
  • Access-layer security should be designed from the beginning, not added later.
  • Simpler operations and smaller failure domains can conflict. Document the trade-off and test the failure behavior.
  • Anonymizing old work is a good way to document experience without exposing customer data.

Design Improvement I Would Apply Today

Looking at this design with more experience, I would separate ISP transport from trusted campus switching: ISP handoffs → dedicated WAN stack → FortiGate HA → campus core/distribution. The WAN stack would carry isolated ISP VLANs to the firewall, with no direct path into the LAN. This adds equipment, but makes the security boundary and troubleshooting sequence easier to follow.

I would separate each firewall's WAN-side and LAN-side LACP aggregates. WAN bundles would terminate on the dedicated WAN stack; LAN bundles would terminate on the campus core/distribution pair. Each switch-side bundle would serve one FortiGate unit rather than combining ports to both HA units into one EtherChannel. VLAN allow-lists and firewall subinterfaces would match the function of each handoff. HA and LACP behavior, including secondary-unit participation, must follow the supported configuration for the selected FortiOS release.

I would size the bundles from required throughput, available interfaces, and capacity after a member or chassis failure. The record does not justify a specific member count. LACP negotiates the bundle; traffic distribution follows the forwarding hash. A single flow does not normally use the sum of all member speeds. On the C9500 StackWise Virtual pair, a bundle spanning both chassis is a MEC. On a C9300 StackWise stack, the corresponding term is cross-stack EtherChannel. Neither design justifies spanning a bundle across two unrelated standalone switches or claiming physical diversity when its members share a cable route.
